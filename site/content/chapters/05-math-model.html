What you should be able to do

  • Write a pure qubit state, normalize it, and read probabilities off it.
  • State the Born rule and the normalization condition.
  • Explain what a "probability subtraction" trick is and why it is useful.
  • Distinguish deterministic from probabilistic operations, and evolution from collapse.

From physics to a model

Von Neumann's model of a measurement has two stages:

  1. Evolution — a deterministic, reversible, unitary transformation (Schrödinger evolution).
  2. Collapse — a non-unitary projection onto an eigenstate of the measurement operator.

The course's "Subtractive Probabilities Through Complex Numbers" and "Modeling Superposition Through Matrices" articles are the two halves of this: the first shows how to get a probability, the second shows how superposition is represented.

The state

A pure qubit state is

\[ |\psi\rangle = \alpha |0\rangle + \beta |1\rangle, \qquad |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1 \]

with the probabilities given by the Born rule:

\[ P(0) = |\alpha|^2, \qquad P(1) = |\beta|^2 \]

Written as a column vector:

\[ |\psi\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix} \]

Subtracting probabilities

Here is the trick the course builds on. Suppose you want the probability of "outcome 0" for a state \(|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\):

\[ P(0) = |\langle 0 | \psi\rangle|^2 \]

The subtractive method works backwards from the total. Since the two outcomes are the only ones available and the state is normalized,

\[ P(0) = 1 - P(1) \]

and \(P(1)\) is obtained by projecting the state onto \(|1\rangle\) and taking the squared modulus. In code this is a single matrix-vector product, and the reason the course insists on the algebra is that the same projection gives you the post-measurement state for free.

Superposition through matrices

Every gate is a \(2\times2\) complex matrix acting on the column vector:

\[ |\psi'\rangle = U |\psi\rangle \]

and the states reachable from \(|0\rangle\) are exactly the columns of \(U\). If the columns of \(U\) are \(|u_0\rangle\) and \(|u_1\rangle\) then

\[ U = \begin{pmatrix} u_{00} & u_{10} \\ u_{01} & u_{11} \end{pmatrix} \]

— the first column is the image of \(|0\rangle\) and the second is the image of \(|1\rangle\). Getting this orientation backwards is the single most common bug in a first quantum program, and lab L08 checks it explicitly.

Deterministic versus probabilistic

Deterministic Probabilistic
Example X gate, Z gate Measurement
Reversible? yes no
Input \(\to\) output one to one many to one
Physics unitary evolution collapse

The whole point of the model is that only measurement is irreversible. Everything else in a quantum program can be undone.
