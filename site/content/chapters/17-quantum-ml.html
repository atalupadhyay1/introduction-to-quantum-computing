The classical KNN

Normalise the vectors, compute the squared Euclidean distance, turn each distance into a weight, and normalise the weights into probabilities:

\[ w_i = 1 - 0.25\, d_i^2, \qquad P(c \mid x) = \frac{\sum_{i \in c} w_i}{\sum_i w_i} \]

On the course's data this gives \(P(1) = 0.5244\) and \(P(0) = 0.4756\).

The quantum KNN, and why it works

The circuit encodes the training vectors and the query as amplitudes, and a Hadamard on a "training vs query" qubit makes the two halves interfere. At a slot holding a training component \(v_i\) and the query component \(q_i\), the amplitude in the training half becomes

\[ \frac{v_i + q_i}{2\sqrt2} \]

and now the identity that makes the whole thing work:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_i (v_i + q_i)^2 &= \sum_i \bigl(v_i^2 + q_i^2 + 2 v_i q_i\bigr) = 2 + 2\sum_i v_i q_i\\ \text{and}\quad w &= 1 - 0.25\,d^2 = 1 - 0.25\bigl(2 - 2\sum_i v_i q_i\bigr) = 0.5\bigl(1 + \sum_i v_i q_i\bigr) \end{aligned} \]

so

\[ \sum_i (v_i + q_i)^2 = 4\,w \]

The interference amplitude is the square root of four times the classical weight. Because the probabilities in each class sum to the same total denominator, the conditional probability of measuring a class is exactly the classical normalised weight.

The lab confirms this three ways: 20 000 shots give \(P(1) = 0.5248\) against the classical \(0.5244\), and the exact statevector answer agrees to machine precision.

Support vector machines

An SVM finds the widest empty strip between two classes. The boundary sits between two hyperplanes \(w \cdot x + b = \pm 1\), the margin is \(2/\lVert w\rVert\), and only the points touching the hyperplanes — the support vectors — constrain the solution.

  • Overfitting: one mislabelled point far out on the wrong side drags the whole boundary with it. The lab shows a clean dataset separated perfectly and a spoiled one that is not.
  • Soft margins: allow violations and charge for them, \(\min \tfrac12\lVert w\rVert^2 + C\sum_i \text{slack}_i\). Big \(C\) means few mistakes and a weird boundary; small \(C\) means mistakes are cheap and the boundary stays smooth.
  • Kernels: \(K(x,z) = \phi(x)\cdot\phi(z)\) lets you use a linear SVM in a high-dimensional feature space without ever building \(\phi\). The polynomial kernel \((x \cdot z + c)^d\) is the standard example — and on the rings dataset, the linear kernel scores 0.50 while the polynomial kernel scores 0.96.
  • Multiple classes: one-vs-rest (one binary SVM per class) or one-vs-one (one per pair).

Quantum support vector machines

The quantum kernel is

\[ K(x, z) = \bigl|\langle \phi(x) \mid \phi(z)\rangle\bigr|^2 \]

with \(\lvert\phi(x)\rangle = U(x)\lvert0\rangle^n\). The course uses the ZZ feature map with 4 features, 2 repetitions and linear entanglement. The lab computes a kernel entry directly from the state vectors, then trains a real QSVC on the iris dataset and reproduces the course's predictions: [5.0, 3.3, 1.5, 0.3] → 0 and [6.0, 2.9, 5.2, 1.7] → 2.

The course's original qsvm.py used BasicAer, QuantumInstance and algorithm_globals, all removed in Qiskit 1.0. The modern equivalents are FidelityQuantumKernel with a QMLSampler, and QSVC(quantum_kernel=...).

The significance — honestly

Claim Reality
"a quantum computer learns faster" not shown; data loading usually dominates
"the quantum kernel is exponentially rich" true in principle, hard to exploit
"you can read out the whole feature map" no — you read out an expectation value
"it is a drop-in replacement" yes: the kernel is the interface
  1. Quantum machine learning is not a speed-up for everything. It is a new way to compute a similarity.
  2. The useful part is the kernel: a quantum circuit can evaluate a function of two data points that is awkward to write down classically.
  3. Everything else — the loss, the optimizer, the train/test split — stays classical, exactly as in section 16.
  4. The real obstacle is hardware: today's devices are noisy, and a deep feature map needs a lot of error correction before it beats a classical kernel machine.
