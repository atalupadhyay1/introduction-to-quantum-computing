Product states versus entangled states

A two-qubit state is a product state if it factorizes:

\[ \lvert\psi\rangle = \lvert\alpha\rangle \otimes \lvert\beta\rangle \]

Everything else is entangled. The canonical example is the Bell state

\[ \lvert\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl(\lvert00\rangle + \lvert11\rangle\bigr) \]

which cannot be written in the product form at all.

The four Bell states

\[ \begin{aligned} \lvert\Phi^+\rangle &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\lvert00\rangle + \lvert11\rangle)\\ \lvert\Phi^-\rangle &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\lvert00\rangle - \lvert11\rangle)\\ \lvert\Psi^+\rangle &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\lvert01\rangle + \lvert10\rangle)\\ \lvert\Psi^-\rangle &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\lvert01\rangle - \lvert10\rangle) \end{aligned} \]

The first two differ in the relative phase of \(|00\rangle\) and \(|11\rangle\); the last two in the relative phase of \(|01\rangle\) and \(|10\rangle\). The phase difference is not observable in the computational basis — you need the \(X\) basis to see it.

Measuring one qubit is enough

Measure \(|\Phi^+\rangle\) in the computational basis. You get \(00\) or \(11\) with equal probability — never \(01\) or \(10\). If the first qubit is measured, the second is instantly determined.

But the second qubit's outcome, on its own, is completely random. That is why entanglement cannot be used to send information faster than light: Bob sees a random bit, and only the joint statistics reveal the correlation.

Reduced states and entropy

To measure the entanglement, trace one qubit out:

\[ \rho_A = \operatorname{Tr}_B \bigl(\lvert\psi\rangle\langle\psi\rvert\bigr) \]

For the Bell state this gives \(\rho_A = \tfrac12 I\) — the maximally mixed state. Its eigenvalues are \(\tfrac12, \tfrac12\), so the entanglement entropy (the von Neumann entropy of the reduced state) is

\[ S = -\sum_i \lambda_i \log_2 \lambda_i = -2 \cdot \tfrac12 \log_2 \tfrac12 = 1 \text{ bit} \]

Worked examples:

state entropy
\(\lvert+\rangle \otimes \lvert+\rangle\) (product) 0 bits
a Bell pair 1 bit
GHZ on \(n\) qubits, any balanced cut 1 bit
a maximally entangled \(n\)-qubit state \(n/2\) bits
two crossed Bell pairs, \((q_0,q_2)\) and \((q_1,q_3)\) 2 bits

That last row is the interesting one. Two parallel Bell pairs (say \(q_0q_1\) and \(q_2q_3\)) give only 1 bit across the \(2|2\) cut, because each pair can be traced out separately. But two crossed Bell pairs have a \(2|2\) cut whose reduced state has two eigenvalues of \(\tfrac12\) each, so the entropy is 2 bits.

The entropy is computed from the eigenvalues of the reduced state, not from its diagonal. The lab checks this explicitly.

CHSH and Bell's theorem

With two parties sharing a pair and each choosing a measurement setting \(\{a_0, a_1\}\) and \(\{b_0, b_1\}\), define the correlation coefficients \(E_{xy} = P(\text{same outcome}) - P(\text{different outcome})\). The CHSH parameter is

\[ S = E_{00} + E_{01} + E_{10} - E_{11} \]
  • Classical (local hidden variable) theories: \(S \le 2\);
  • Quantum mechanics: \(S \le 2\sqrt2 \approx 2.83\).

Experiments close to the quantum bound rule out local hidden-variable theories — which is the deepest practical payoff of entanglement.
