Section 7 — Modeling Quantum Spin States with Math

Course section 7 of 17 · Lab L10 · Quiz 6 questions

What you should be able to do

  • Write a spin state as a column vector and apply a gate to it.
  • Use Dirac notation fluently: bras, kets, inner and outer products.
  • Write and use the Hadamard, \(X\), \(Y\) and \(Z\) gates.
  • Read probabilities off a state vector with the Born rule.

Column matrix representation

A pure spin state is a column vector of complex amplitudes:

\[ |\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad \begin{pmatrix}\alpha \\ \beta\end{pmatrix}, \qquad |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1 \]

A mixed state is a density matrix \(\rho = |\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\), but you do not need it until the course's later sections.

Dirac notation

Notation Meaning
\(\lvert\psi\rangle\) ket — a state
\(\langle\psi\rvert\) bra — the adjoint of the ket
\(\langle\phi\lvert\psi\rangle\) inner product — a complex number
\(\lvert\phi\rangle\langle\psi\rvert\) outer product — an operator
\(\langle\psi\lvert\psi\rangle\) 1 for a normalized state

The adjoint rule is the one to memorise: \(\langle\psi| = \psi^\dagger\), and for a column vector \(\psi = (\psi_1, \psi_2)^T\) the bra is the row vector \((\psi_1^*, \psi_2^*)\).

The gates

\[ H = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad X = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad Y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad Z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix} \]

plus the phase gates

\[ S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & i\end{pmatrix}, \qquad T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\pi/4}\end{pmatrix} \]

Each one does something specific to the Bloch sphere:

Gate Action
\(X\) rotate 180° about the \(x\) axis (bit flip)
\(Y\) rotate 180° about the \(y\) axis
\(Z\) rotate 180° about the \(z\) axis (phase flip)
\(H\) rotate 90° about the axis \((\hat{x} + \hat{z})/\sqrt2\)

Two facts worth checking yourself:

  • \(H|0\rangle = |+\rangle = (|0\rangle + |1\rangle)/\sqrt2\) and \(H|1\rangle = |-\rangle = (|0\rangle - |1\rangle)/\sqrt2\);
  • \(H^2 = I\), so \(H\) is its own inverse;
  • \(X\), \(Y\) and \(Z\) are Hermitian and unitary, so \(X^2 = Y^2 = Z^2 = I\).

The operator picture

The alternative to "apply a matrix to a vector" is the operator picture: a gate is an operator that acts on a ket and produces a new ket,

\[ \hat{A}\lvert\psi\rangle = \lvert\psi'\rangle \]

with matrix elements \(\langle\phi|\hat{A}|\psi\rangle\) giving the transition amplitudes. The two pictures are the same thing; the operator picture just makes the symmetry arguments easier to see.

A useful identity: the adjoint of an operator reverses the order and conjugates the bras and kets,

\[ \langle\psi|\hat{A}^\dagger|\phi\rangle = \langle\hat{A}\psi|\phi\rangle \]

which is what lets you "undo" a gate by applying its adjoint.

The Bloch sphere

Every pure qubit state is a point on the unit sphere. The map from \((\theta, \phi)\) to amplitudes is

\[ \alpha = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}, \qquad \beta = e^{i\phi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2} \]

so the \(z\)-coordinate of the sphere point is \(z = |\alpha|^2 - |\beta|^2 = P(0) - P(1)\), and the transverse projection has length \(2|\alpha||\beta| = 2\sqrt{P(0)P(1)}\). Measuring the state in the computational basis gives \(P(0) = (1 + z)/2\).
