The protocol (BB84, 1984)

Bennett and Brassard named their protocol after the year. Each round:

  1. Alice picks a random bit \(b \in \{0,1\}\) and a random basis \(B \in \{Z, X\}\).
  2. Alice sends a photon prepared in that basis: \(Z\) (rectilinear, 0°/90°) or \(X\) (diagonal, 45°/135°).
  3. Bob picks a random basis \(B'\) and measures.
  4. Alice and Bob publicly announce their bases (never the bit values).
  5. They keep only the rounds where \(B = B'\); those bits form the key.

The security rests on one fact: measuring in the wrong basis destroys the information. A photon in state \(|+\rangle\) measured in the \(Z\) basis gives \(0\) or \(1\) with equal probability, no matter what it "really" was.

The quantum bit error rate

Let \(Q\) be the fraction of sifted bits that disagree. This is the number that reveals an eavesdropper.

Scenario QBER
No eavesdropper \(0\%\)
Eve intercepts and resends in the basis she measured in (the textbook attack) \(25\%\)
Eve intercepts and resends in a random basis \(37.5\%\)

Where 25% comes from. Eve measures each photon in a basis of her own choosing and resends a fresh photon in that basis.

  • With probability \(\tfrac12\) she happens to pick Alice's basis, so she measures the bit correctly and resends it correctly — no error.
  • With probability \(\tfrac12\) she picks the other basis, so her result is random, and Bob's reading of her resend is random too — a 50% error rate.
\[ Q = \tfrac12 \cdot 0 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac14 = 25\% \]

Where 37.5% comes from. A noisier but stronger attack: Eve resends in a random basis instead of the one she measured in. Then

  • \(\tfrac14\) of the time she measures Alice's basis and resends in Alice's basis: no error at all;
  • \(\tfrac14\) of the time she measures the wrong basis but resends in Alice's basis: her bit is random, so a 50% error rate;
  • \(\tfrac12\) of the time she resends in the wrong basis: Bob's reading is random, so a 50% error rate.
\[ Q = \tfrac14 \cdot 0 + \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac38 = 37.5\% \]

Both are far above the threshold, so both are caught.

The threshold. In a one-way BB84 system, aborting when \(Q > 11\%\) is the standard rule. The bound is \(Q_{\text{intercept}} \approx 0.1464\), and the abort threshold is roughly \(\frac{2}{3}\) of that.

The one-time pad

BB84 only gives you a shared random key. To encrypt a message with it you need the one-time pad:

\[ C = M \oplus K, \qquad M = C \oplus K \]

It is unconditionally secure — secure regardless of how much computation the eavesdropper can do — but only if:

  • the key is used once and only once;
  • the key is uniformly random;
  • the key is the same length as the message;
  • the key is shared secretly.

Reuse a key and XOR-ing the two ciphertexts leaks the XOR of the two plaintexts. That is exactly how the Vigenère cipher falls.
