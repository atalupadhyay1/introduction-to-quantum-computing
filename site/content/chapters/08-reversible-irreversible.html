Reversible means invertible

A gate \(U\) is reversible because

\[ U^\dagger U = I \quad\Longrightarrow\quad U^{-1} = U^\dagger \]

so you can always undo it. Concretely: \(H^{-1} = H\), \(X^{-1} = X\), \(T^{-1} = T^\dagger = T^3\).

The corollary is the one that matters for computing: you cannot have a quantum program that computes a function and then forgets the input. Any classical function you want to compute with a quantum circuit must be written so that the input is preserved, typically in extra "ancilla" qubits.

The lab's XOR construction is exactly this. With an ancilla \(C = |0\rangle\):

\[ \text{Toffoli}(A,B;C)\;\to\;\text{Toffoli}(A,B;C)\;\to\;\text{CNOT}(B \to A) \]

computes \((A \oplus B, B, 0)\) — the XOR of the two inputs, with the original inputs intact and the ancilla returned to \(|0\rangle\). The first Toffoli computes \(A \land B\) into \(C\), the second uncomputes it (which is possible because the Toffoli is its own inverse), and the CNOT does the XOR using the copy.

Irreversible operations

Operation Irreversible? Why
Any unitary gate no it has an inverse
Measurement yes many-to-one, information is destroyed
Reset to $ 0\rangle$ yes
Preparation of a state yes the state you prepared is gone

Measurement is modelled by a projector \(P = |0\rangle\langle0|\) (or its partner). The post-measurement state is

\[ \lvert\psi'\rangle = \frac{P\lvert\psi\rangle}{\lVert P\lvert\psi\rangle\rVert} \]

— the normalized projection, which is why the post-measurement state is always a basis state (or a superposition within a degenerate subspace).

No-cloning

The theorem. There is no unitary operation that maps \(|\psi\rangle \mapsto |\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle\) for every unknown state \(|\psi\rangle\).

The proof sketch. If such a \(U\) existed, then \(U(|0\rangle|0\rangle) = |00\rangle\) and \(U(|1\rangle|1\rangle) = |11\rangle\). By linearity, \(U(|+\rangle|+\rangle) = \frac12(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)\). But cloning should give \(|+\rangle|+\rangle = \frac12(|00\rangle + |01\rangle + |10\rangle + |11\rangle)\). These differ, so no such \(U\) exists.

Why it matters. It is the reason quantum cryptography is interesting (an eavesdropper cannot copy the qubits and keep a perfect copy) and the reason quantum error correction is hard (you cannot back up a qubit).

Note that the theorem is about unknown states: you can clone a known state, and you can clone a whole basis consistently, which is exactly what the classical "intercept and resend" attack does.

Different bases, different answers

Two states can be perfectly distinguishable in one basis and completely indistinguishable in another:

\[ |+\rangle = \frac{|0\rangle + |1\rangle}{\sqrt2}, \qquad |-\rangle = \frac{|0\rangle - |1\rangle}{\sqrt2} \]

Both give 50/50 in the \(Z\) basis; they are perfectly distinguishable in the \(X\) basis (measure with \(H\) first, then in \(Z\)). The overlap \(\langle +|-\rangle = 0\) is the right measure of "how different" two states are, and it is the quantity the quantum kernel computes in section 17.
