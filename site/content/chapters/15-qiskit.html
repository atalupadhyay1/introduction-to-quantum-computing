The difference between to_gate() and to_instruction() matters: a gate is reversible and can be controlled; an instruction is not, and decompose() expands it back into its parts.

BB84 in Qiskit

The course's bb84.py uses a quantum random number generator for every random choice — the bit and the basis:

circuit = QuantumCircuit(1, 1)
circuit.x(0)          # if the bit is 1
circuit.barrier()
circuit.h(0)          # if the basis is diagonal
circuit.barrier()
circuit.measure(0, 0)

The barrier() calls are cosmetic but instructive: they stop the optimiser from merging the gates across the random choice, which would change the physics.

Shor's algorithm

Shor's algorithm reduces factoring to finding the period \(r\) of

\[ f(x) = a^x \bmod N \]

for a random \(a\) coprime to \(N\). The classical part of the reduction is easy; the hard part — computing a discrete logarithm — is where the quantum part enters.

The quantum circuit is Kitaev's order-finding circuit:

  1. the counting register (\(m\) qubits) goes into a uniform superposition;
  2. the target register is prepared in \(\lvert1\rangle\);
  3. for each counting qubit \(i\), apply a controlled \(U^{2^i}\), where \(U\lvert y\rangle = a \cdot y \bmod N\);
  4. apply the inverse QFT to the counting register;
  5. measure the counting register.

The measurement lands on multiples of \(2^m / r\), so \(r\) falls out of the distribution, recovered by a continued-fraction expansion of \(y/2^m\).

The lab implements exactly this with \(N = 15\), \(a = 2\), \(m = 4\). The classical period is 4, and the quantum run gives peaks at \(0, 4, 8, 12\) — the multiples of \(16/4 = 4\) — and recovers \(r = 4\). Then

\[ \gcd\bigl(2^{4/2} - 1, 15\bigr) = \gcd(3, 15) = 3 \]

and 3 is a factor of 15.
