Photons are the friendliest quantum system

A photon of light travelling in a fixed direction has exactly two independent polarization states:

  • horizontal (H)
  • vertical (V)

That is all you need. A photon is the qubit of this section, with \(|0\rangle \equiv \mathrm{H}\) and \(|1\rangle \equiv \mathrm{V}\).

Malus's law

Send a photon polarized at angle \(\theta_p\) into a polarizer whose transmission axis is at angle \(\theta_f\). The probability it passes is

\[ P(\text{pass}) = \cos^2(\theta_p - \theta_f) \]

and the probability it is absorbed is

\[ P(\text{block}) = \sin^2(\theta_p - \theta_f) \]

Two special cases are worth memorising:

Setup \(P(\text{pass})\)
Same angle (\(\theta_p = \theta_f\)) \(1\)
90° apart \(0\)
45° apart \(\tfrac12\)

Worked example: a photon at \(0^\circ\) through a polarizer at \(60^\circ\) gives \(P(\text{pass}) = \cos^2 60^\circ = 0.25\).

Superposition, and the part that is genuinely quantum

A polarizer at \(45^\circ\) transmits a photon that is in the state

\[ |+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\bigl(|0\rangle + |1\rangle\bigr) \]

and a polarizer at \(-45^\circ\) transmits

\[ |-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\bigl(|0\rangle - |1\rangle\bigr) \]

A classical wave can also be split between two directions, and the interference pattern is the same. The difference appears when you measure the polarization:

  • a classical wave keeps both components and shows them in the intensity;
  • a quantum photon is found in one definite polarization, chosen at random, with probabilities \(|\alpha|^2\) and \(|\beta|^2\).

That irreversible, probabilistic collapse is what makes it quantum, and it is exactly the Born rule you will formalise in section 5.

The cascade

Three polarizers in a row is the classic demonstration. With \(0^\circ \rightarrow 45^\circ \rightarrow 90^\circ\):

Stage Conditional probability of passing
0° (input already 0°) \(1\)
45° \(\cos^2 45^\circ = 0.5\)
90° \(\cos^2 45^\circ = 0.5\)

Total probability of emerging: \(0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25\). But remove the middle polarizer and the probability is zero — the \(45^\circ\) polarizer is what created the superposition that the \(90^\circ\) polarizer then destroyed. This is interference, and it is the same phenomenon that underlies every quantum algorithm.
